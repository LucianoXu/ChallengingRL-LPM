\begin{abstract}
\noindent These notes cover the project end to end --- both halves of \texttt{docs/SPEC.md}. \textbf{Part~I
(SPEC~\S2, slide deck \S2) reproduces the LPM paper} on its own 3D \textbf{MiniWorld} maze: 6 methods
$\times$ 3 noise variants $\times$ 64 seeds, A2C, the paper's $5\!\times\!10^4$-step budget, with a
paper-faithful log-space LPM. The headline noisy-TV robustness claim \emph{reproduces} (under
\texttt{action\_noise} the raw-pixel predictor MSE fixates on the noise wall $83\%$ of the time and its
coverage collapses, while LPM almost never looks at it, $3\%$); but the paper's ``LPM achieves the
highest coverage'' ranking does \emph{not} --- a \emph{uniform-random} control covers the most in all
three variants. The lesson: \textbf{coverage is the wrong yardstick in an extrinsic-free maze}, which
motivates Part~II. \textbf{Part~II (SPEC~\S3, slide deck \S3) is our extension to sparse-reward
MiniGrid}, where a real task reward lets intrinsic motivation pay off and \emph{task success}
discriminates exploration quality. Under a common PPO agent (MLP on the $7\times7$ symbolic view) we
compare a plain baseline (\textsc{none}), entropy-bonus exploration (\textsc{entropy}), Random Network
Distillation (\textsc{rnd}) and the paper's Learning Progress Monitoring (\textsc{lpm}) across a
three-rung difficulty ladder (easy DoorKey-5x5, medium FourRooms, hard MultiRoom-N6), clean and under
observation noise. Three results. \textbf{(1)} The intrinsic coefficient $\beta$ matters enormously and
is environment-dependent: $\beta{=}0.05$ \emph{drowns} the sparse reward; the usable range is
$\sim$0.001--0.005. \textbf{(2)} Intrinsic motivation is \emph{difficulty-gated}: unnecessary on
easy/medium, but \textbf{decisive on hard MultiRoom-N6, where RND solves the task (eval $0.32$) while
baseline/entropy never reach the goal}. \textbf{(3)} That trade-off reverses under noise: on noisy
DoorKey-5x5 \textbf{RND collapses while LPM stays robust} --- a clean MiniGrid reproduction of the
paper's noise-robustness claim in a second, symbolic-observation domain. Figures, tables and agent
traces below are mapped to the planned slide deck.
\end{abstract}

\part*{Part~I --- Reproducing the LPM paper on MiniWorld (slide deck \S2)}
\addcontentsline{toc}{part}{Part I: MiniWorld reproduction}

\section{MiniWorld environment and setup (slides 2.1--2.2)}\label{sec:mwsetup}
\textbf{Environment.} The paper's exploration testbed is a hand-designed 3D \textbf{MiniWorld} maze
(four rooms, $160\times120$ RGB first-person observations) with \emph{no extrinsic reward} --- the
agent is scored purely by how much of the maze it visits. Two ``noisy-TV'' variants stress-test
robustness: \texttt{noisy\_tv}, where one wall shows white noise (a green-pixel $\to$ random-RGB
transform), and \texttt{action\_noise}, where a dedicated action (\#4) replaces the whole observation
with a random CIFAR-10 image --- an unlearnable, perpetually ``novel'' distractor that a
prediction-error bonus will chase forever. \texttt{nonoise} is the clean control.

\textbf{Agent, methods, metrics.} A2C (the upstream pipeline), shared CNN encoder, $5\!\times\!10^4$
exploration steps per run --- the paper's maze budget (Fig.~3 of Hou et al.). We compare \textbf{six}
policies: \textsc{lpm} (paper-faithful log-space learning-progress reward
$r^i=g_\phi-\log\mathrm{MSE}$), \textsc{rnd}, \textsc{icm}, \textsc{mse} (raw next-frame pixel error,
the pure noisy-TV victim), \textsc{none} (A2C, no intrinsic reward), and a \emph{uniform-random}
control ($a\sim\mathrm{Uniform}\{0\dots4\}$, no learning --- the ``does any of this beat chance?''
baseline). Metrics: \textbf{coverage} (fraction of reachable cells visited), \textbf{TV-action share}
(fraction of steps spent on the noise action under \texttt{action\_noise} --- the direct fixation
measure), and \textbf{coverage-heatmap evolution} (per-cell occupancy over training).
\note{We made LPM paper-faithful: log-space Eq.~3 instead of the released notebooks' raw-error clipped
form, which silently masked a stability issue. Full design + numbers:
\texttt{latex\_notes/2026-05-31-maze-exploration-design.pdf}; slides: \texttt{reports/report2/}.}

\paragraph{Conclusion: coverage is not a good exploration metric (slide 2.5).} In this small,
extrinsic-free maze, coverage rewards undirected \emph{randomness} --- uniform thrashing spreads
through the rooms while any \emph{learned} policy commits to a narrower, repetitive walk, so training
\emph{reduces} coverage. Noise-avoidance and coverage are decoupled (under \texttt{action\_noise}
uniform wastes $20\%$ of steps frozen on the wall yet still covers most; LPM never touches the wall yet
covers less on average). \textbf{So what \emph{is} a good metric for exploration?} Our answer, and the
pivot of the project: move to a setting with a real, sparse \emph{task} reward, and measure
\emph{success at the task}. That is Part~II. \note{Slide 2.5 --- the bridge to the MiniGrid work.}

\section{Setup}\label{sec:mgsetup}
\textbf{Observation space.} A MiniGrid step returns a dictionary
$\{\texttt{image}, \texttt{direction}, \texttt{mission}\}$. The \texttt{image} is the agent's
\emph{egocentric, partially-observable} $7\times7$ view of the cells \emph{in front of it} (not the
full grid), with \textbf{3 channels} per cell encoding $(\text{object id},\ \text{colour id},\
\text{state})$ in MiniGrid's compact \emph{symbolic} scheme --- e.g.\ object$\in\{$wall, door, key,
goal, $\dots\}$, state$\in\{$open, closed, locked$\}$ for doors. These are \emph{not} RGB pixels. We
wrap with \texttt{ImgObs} (keep only the image) and flatten to a \textbf{147-dim}
($=7\!\times\!7\!\times\!3$) vector. \note{This answers ``why were the observations 2835-dim?'': the
default \texttt{FlatObs} \emph{appends} a one-hot encoding of the mission string to the image, and that
mission is fixed per task --- so $\sim$95\% of \texttt{FlatObs} is a constant, uninformative padding.
\texttt{ImgObs} carries the identical spatial information $\sim$19$\times$ lighter; we use it
throughout.}

\textbf{Action space.} MiniGrid's native action space is $\mathrm{Discrete}(7)$:
$\{0\!:\!\text{turn left},\ 1\!:\!\text{turn right},\ 2\!:\!\text{move forward},\ 3\!:\!\text{pickup},\
4\!:\!\text{drop},\ 5\!:\!\text{toggle/open},\ 6\!:\!\text{done}\}$. We restrict it to the minimal
useful subset \emph{per environment} (a \texttt{MiniGridActionSubsetWrapper}), which removes dead
actions and shrinks the exploration branching factor: \textbf{FourRooms} (pure navigation) uses
$\{$left, right, forward$\}$ (\textbf{3} actions); \textbf{DoorKey} and \textbf{MultiRoom-N6} (must
grab a key / open doors) use $\{$left, right, forward, pickup, toggle$\}$ (\textbf{5} actions); the
never-needed \texttt{drop} and \texttt{done} are dropped.

\textbf{Model and PPO hyperparameters.} PPO (Stable-Baselines3), \texttt{MlpPolicy} (the SB3 default:
separate actor and critic MLPs, two hidden layers of $64$ with $\tanh$), $\mathbf{8}$ parallel envs.
Rollout $\texttt{n\_steps}{=}512$ per env ($\Rightarrow$ a $8\!\times\!512{=}4096$-transition buffer
per update), $\texttt{batch\_size}{=}64$, $\texttt{n\_epochs}{=}10$, $\texttt{learning\_rate}{=}2.5\mathrm{e}{-}4$,
$\gamma{=}0.99$, GAE $\lambda{=}0.95$, $\texttt{clip\_range}{=}0.2$, $\texttt{ent\_coef}{=}0$ (the
\textsc{entropy} arm overrides this to $0.01$). Deterministic evaluation every $5\mathrm{k}$ steps over
$10$ episodes (each a freshly randomized layout; see \emph{Procedural layout randomization} below) on a
clean-of-intrinsic copy of the env. The intrinsic reward enters the \emph{training}
return as $r = r^{\text{ext}} + \beta\,r^{i}_{\text{norm}}$; eval return is the pure sparse task reward.

\textbf{Exploration methods.} (i) \textsc{none}: plain PPO, $\mathrm{ent\_coef}{=}0$. (ii)
\textsc{entropy}: PPO with $\mathrm{ent\_coef}{=}0.01$ --- the \emph{non-intrinsic} (policy-stochasticity)
comparator. (iii) \textsc{rnd}: bonus $=\lVert f_\theta(s)-\hat f(s)\rVert^2$ (predictor vs.\ fixed
random target). (iv) \textsc{lpm}: paper-faithful log-space reward $r^i=g_\phi-\log\mathrm{MSE}$, gated
until the error buffer fills.

\textbf{Environments (difficulty ladder, one per tier).} easy \texttt{DoorKey-5x5} (subgoal shaping
disabled $\to$ purely sparse), medium \texttt{FourRooms}, hard \texttt{MultiRoom-N6}.
\note{KeyCorridor-S3R3 was tried as the hard rung and abandoned: it requires \emph{memory} (carry a
key, remember it, open a door), which a feed-forward MLP on the image-only view cannot represent ---
all methods scored $0$ even at 3M steps. MultiRoom-N6 is memory-free (doors just toggle) so it is
MLP-solvable, but exploration-hard.}

\textbf{Procedural layout randomization (every episode).} All three environments
\emph{regenerate their layout on every reset} --- during training \emph{and} during evaluation; the
random seed only fixes the RNG stream, not a single fixed maze. The tiers differ in \emph{how much}
they vary. \texttt{DoorKey-5x5} is only mildly random: the structure is fixed (agent in the left
room, one door in the dividing wall, key in the left room, and the \textbf{goal pinned in the far
corner}), with just the key/door/spawn cells jittering by a step or two. \texttt{FourRooms} places
\textbf{both the spawn and the goal uniformly at random} across the four rooms, so a single episode can
be trivial (same room, a few cells apart) or demand crossing all four. \texttt{MultiRoom-N6} draws a
fresh $6$-room chain each episode (random room sizes, placements and door positions) with the goal in
the final room --- always far from the spawn. Consequently \textbf{every reported eval return is an
average over this layout distribution}, not a single-maze score: this is the representative way to
measure generalisation, and it is why FourRooms' mean sits low (it averages a wide spread of easy and
hard goal placements). \note{A fixed-seed render therefore shows only \emph{one} draw from this
distribution: the agent-trace film-strips (\S\ref{sec:traces}) are single representative episodes,
chosen for clarity rather than as summaries of the layout distribution. The caveat bites hardest for
FourRooms, where a fixed seed can land a trivial same-room layout; DoorKey/MultiRoom are safer because
their goal is \emph{structurally} far from the spawn (fixed far corner / end of the room chain).}

\textbf{Metrics, seeds, budget.} Final deterministic eval return (the sparse task reward; the eval
env carries no intrinsic bonus), the per-episode extrinsic/intrinsic split, and sample-efficiency
curves. Mean$\pm$std over seeds: \textbf{8 seeds on DoorKey-5x5} (bumped up to resolve LPM's high
variance there; see \S\ref{sec:rq4}), \textbf{3 seeds} elsewhere. Budgets: easy/medium 1M, hard 2M
PPO steps.

\section{$\beta$: how to mix extrinsic and intrinsic reward (RQ2)}\label{sec:beta}
\begin{figure}[h]\centering\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{fig2_beta_sweep.png}
\caption{$\beta$ sweep on FourRooms (500k). RND rises to a sweet spot ($\beta\!\approx\!0.001$--$0.005$,
matching the baseline) then collapses to $0$ at $\beta{=}0.05$; LPM declines monotonically.}
\label{fig:beta}\end{figure}

At $\beta{=}0.05$ the per-episode \emph{intrinsic} reward was $100$--$700\times$ the sparse extrinsic
reward, so the policy optimises novelty and never learns the goal (eval $\to 0$). The usable band is
$\sim$0.001--0.005. On the \emph{hard} env a slightly larger $\beta$ is needed to drive exploration,
but too large still prevents convergence to exploitation: RND on MultiRoom-N6 only converges at
$\beta{=}0.005$ (eval $0.28$); at $\beta{=}0.01$/$0.05$ it reaches the goal during training but never
settles into a reliable deterministic policy (eval $\approx 0$). \textbf{Takeaway:} $\beta$ is
environment-dependent --- small when intrinsic reward is a nuisance, larger when it is essential, with
a sweet spot in between. \note{Slide 3.3.}

Intrinsic motivation's benefit appears \emph{only} when exploration is genuinely hard. On easy and
medium tasks the PPO baseline explores adequately on its own and any bonus slightly degrades it; on
hard MultiRoom-N6 the baseline and entropy never reach the goal, while \textbf{RND solves it reliably}
(0.32, all seeds). \note{Slide 3.4.}

\paragraph{Why RND $>$ LPM on clean hard exploration.} Per-episode intrinsic magnitudes show the
mechanism: RND's bonus is \emph{always positive} (mean $=$ abs), a consistent directional drive toward
novel states; LPM's bonus is \emph{signed} and nets $\approx 0$ per episode (mean $+0.08$ vs.\ abs
$0.33$), giving no coherent exploration push --- so the LPM agent behaves like the baseline and never
explores out. Raising $\beta$ scales LPM's oscillation amplitude, not its directionality, so no
$\beta$ rescues it. This is LPM's designed trade-off: its learning-progress signal goes to $0$ both
for unpredictable noise (its robustness virtue, next section) \emph{and} for already-learned local
dynamics (so it under-explores clean hard tasks).

Observation noise makes every state look ``novel,'' so RND's prediction-error bonus stays high and the
agent chases noise instead of the goal; LPM's learning-progress signal stays near $0$ on unlearnable
noise, so LPM is not distracted and effectively reverts to the (working) base policy. DoorKey-5x5
(Table~\ref{tab:doorkeynoise}) isolates this cleanly because the task is easy enough that noisy
perception alone does not break it. On FourRooms the same \emph{ordering} holds (RND hurt most, LPM
least, Fig.~\ref{fig:noise} right) but the global noise also degrades the policy's perception, so all
methods fall to $\approx 0.05$ --- ``LPM degrades less,'' not ``LPM stays solved.'' On hard
MultiRoom-N6, noise breaks perception for everyone (RND's clean $0.32 \to 0$). \note{Slides 3.4/3.6.}

\paragraph{LPM-clean variance is bimodality, not sampling noise (8-seed check).} The one place LPM's
$3$-seed band looked alarmingly wide was \emph{clean} DoorKey-5x5. We bumped it to \textbf{8 seeds} to
check whether more data would tighten it. It does not: the per-seed final returns are
$\{0.97,0.96,0.97,0.97,0.97,0.87\}$ for six seeds and $\{0.00,0.00\}$ for two, i.e.\ LPM \emph{either}
solves the task ($\approx0.96$) \emph{or} under-explores and never reaches the goal ($0$), with no
middle ground (mean $0.67$, std $0.41$). Adding seeds sharpened this picture rather than shrinking the
std --- the variance is \emph{intrinsic bimodality}, not small-sample uncertainty. This is the same
signature as the MiniWorld result (Part~I): the faithful, unclipped $\lambda_i{=}1$ LPM reward is
noise-robust but prone to a per-seed policy collapse. RND, by contrast, is rock-solid on the same task
($8/8$ seeds at $\approx0.965$, std $0.001$) --- its always-positive bonus gives a stable exploration
drive, where LPM's net-zero signal can leave a seed stuck. \note{Reporting this honestly is the point;
the wide band is a property of LPM, not a measurement defect.}

\section{Conclusions --- answers to the research questions}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item[\textbf{RQ1}] \emph{What do the LPM-paper experiments say about exploration metrics?}
Coverage (MiniWorld) is a poor metric in extrinsic-free settings (a uniform-random policy maximises
it). In MiniGrid we use \emph{task success / return}, which actually discriminates exploration quality.
\item[\textbf{RQ2}] \emph{Correct way to mix extrinsic/intrinsic; $\beta$ too small/large?}
$\beta$ too large drowns the sparse reward ($\to 0$); too small is ignored. Usable $\sim$0.001--0.005,
environment-dependent; on hard tasks there is a sweet spot ($\approx0.005$ for RND) between
under-exploring and never-converging (Fig.~\ref{fig:beta}).
\item[\textbf{RQ3}] \emph{Does intrinsic reward beat baselines on sparse MiniGrid?}
Only when exploration is hard: \textbf{yes, decisively, on MultiRoom-N6 (RND $0.32$ vs.\ $0.0$)}; no on
easy/medium where the baseline suffices (Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder}). RND $>$ LPM on clean hard exploration.
\item[\textbf{RQ4}] \emph{Does LPM generalise to other noise (beyond the MiniWorld noisy-TV)?}
Yes --- \textbf{LPM is noise-robust and RND noise-vulnerable}, cleanest on DoorKey-5x5
(RND $0.87\!\to\!0.03$, LPM $0.67\!\to\!0.93$; Table~\ref{tab:doorkeynoise}). This reproduces the paper's
claim in a second, symbolic-observation domain.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{One-line story for the deck:} \emph{intrinsic motivation buys you hard-exploration
ability (RND) at the price of noise-fragility; LPM trades exploration aggressiveness for
noise-robustness} --- and $\beta$ must be tuned or it drowns the task.

\textbf{Compute note:} the 128-core box SIGKILLs any single process after $\sim$18~min, so long runs
use chunked checkpoint--resume training (\texttt{train\_one --chunk-steps}, resume via
\texttt{PPO.load}) driven by a meta-loop re-invoking \texttt{run\_grid.py} (\texttt{ThreadPoolExecutor});
each chunk finishes under the limit and the idle driver survives. Grids:
\texttt{run\_grid.py --envs ... --methods rnd lpm --betas ... --noise-probs ... --seeds 1 2 3}.
\textbf{Caveats:} (a) the MiniGrid noise is \emph{global} per-element corruption, not a localised
noisy-TV distractor; a localised variant would be a cleaner analogue. (b) LPM under-explores clean
hard tasks here, so the clean LPM-vs-RND gap is RND-favourable; the noise result is the LPM-favourable
half. (c) Seeds: DoorKey-5x5 at 8 seeds (clean) confirms LPM-clean's wide band is \emph{genuine
bimodality} ($6/8$ solve, $2/8$ collapse), not small-sample noise; the noisy DoorKey cells and all
other envs are at 3 seeds (the noisy 8-seed bump hit a \texttt{SubprocVecEnv}+noise spawn crash and
is pending a \texttt{DummyVecEnv} re-run).
